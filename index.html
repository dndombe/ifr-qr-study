Pilot Qualifications

When do you actually need an instrument rating?§61.3

Four situations. If any one of them is true, you need the rating.

  • Acting as PIC under IFR, or in weather conditions less than prescribed for VFR. §61.3
  • Carrying passengers for compensation or hire — on cross-country flights in excess of 50 NM, or at night. §61.133
  • For flight in Class A airspace. §91.135
  • For Special VFR between sunset and sunrise. §91.157

Logging instrument time — who gets to write it down?§61.51

Only the pilot actually flying the aircraft by reference to instruments may log the time — plus, in one narrow case, the instructor.

  • A person may log instrument time only for that flight time when the person operates the aircraft solely by reference to instruments, under actual or simulated instrument flight conditions.
  • An authorized instructor may log instrument time when conducting instrument flight instruction in actual instrument flight conditions.
Don't get caught The pilot's version says actual or simulated. The instructor's version says actual only.
Where to find it
§61.51FAR Part 61, Subpart A — Pilot logbooks

  • An instrument approach at each airport

The flight review — to act as PIC at all§61.56

A flight review is required within the preceding 24 calendar months — precisely, since the beginning of the 24th calendar month before the month of the flight — in an aircraft for which you are rated.

What it consists of

  • A minimum of 1 hour of flight training and 1 hour of ground training
  • Conducted by an authorized instructor

What can be substituted for it

  • A proficiency check or practical pilot test for a pilot certificate, rating, or operating privilege — conducted by an approved examiner, pilot check airman, or US Armed Forces.
  • A practical test, conducted by an examiner, for a flight instructor certificate, additional rating, renewal, or reinstatement.
  • Completion of one or more phases of the FAA-sponsored pilot proficiency award program (WINGS).
  • Flight instructor renewal under Part 61.197 — this exempts the pilot from the ground portion of the flight review only.

Using a simulator for it

A flight simulator or FTD may be used to meet the flight review requirements as long as:

  • It is used in an approved course by a training center under Part 142, and
  • It represents an aircraft for which the pilot is rated.

Lapsed currency — the grace period and the safety pilot§61.57(c)

No "6 HITS" logged within the preceding 6 calendar months? You have an additional 6 calendar months to regain currency by performing the "6 HITS" with a safety pilot, or in an FFS, FTD, or ATD.

Safety pilot requirements

  • Holds at least a private pilot certificate with the appropriate category and class and a valid medical / BasicMed.
  • Has adequate vision forward and to each side of the aircraft.
  • The aircraft must have a dual control system.

More than 6 calendar months since IFR current?

  • An Instrument Proficiency Check (IPC) is required.
  • Administered by a CFII, examiner, or other approved person. Guidelines are in the ACS.
  • Some IPC tasks, but not all, can be conducted in an FTD or ATD. (See ACS)

What must be recorded in the logbook

To meet recent instrument experience requirements, the following must be recorded:

  • Location and type of each instrument approach accomplished, and
  • The name of the safety pilot, if required.

Count backwards from the month of the flight. Which band you land in decides what you may fly.

NOT CURRENT (grace period)

6 calendar months back6 mo 12 calendar months back12 mo month of the flight0
You are current

In the previous 6 calendar months you performed the 6 HITS, or had an IPC, or took the instrument checkride.

It must have been in an appropriate aircraft for the instrument rating to be exercised.

Not current — 6 calendar months to fix it

Nothing logged in the previous 6 calendar months. You now have 6 additional calendar months to regain currency.

IFR recency exemptions for Part 121 / 135 pilots§61.57

A PIC who is actively employed by a Part 121 or 135 operator is exempt from the IFR recency of experience requirements of Part 61.57 when operating under Part 91, 121, or 135 (as applicable) for that operator — as long as they comply with the recency of experience requirements applicable for that operation.

For example, a Part 121 airline pilot does not need to log "6 HITS" to operate under Part 91 or 121 for that airline, as long as you are an active pilot with the company and current with that airline's FAA approved training schedule.

Don't get caught However, you are not IFR current for flying outside the airline unless you also comply with the 6 HITS within 6 calendar months rule.

Logging an instrument approach — the 5 requirements§61.51(g)(1)

Five conditions. Meet them and the approach counts.

1Solely by reference to instrumentsYou must operate the aircraft, FFS, ATD or FTD solely by reference to instruments. A flight instructor may log an IAP conducted by the student in actual IMC (see FAA letter to Levy, 2008).
2Established on each segment, down to minimumsBe established on each required segment of the IAP down to its published minimums (MDA or DA). The Initial (starting at the IAF), Intermediate and Final approach segments are required for logging the approach, unless when radar vectored to final by ATC. In simulated IMC, vectors from the safety pilot are also accepted.
3Simulated conditions continue to minimumsIf conducted in simulated IMC in an aircraft, or in an FFS, ATD or FTD, simulated conditions must continue down to MDA or DA.
4In an aircraft, it must be IMCIn an aircraft, flight must be in actual or simulated IMC.
5Breaking out on final is OKIn an aircraft, if conditions change from actual IMC to VMC during the final approach segment, you can still log the approach.
Don't get caught In simulated IMC the hood is under your control, so it must stay on to minimums. In actual IMC the weather is not under your control, so breaking out on final does not cost you the approach.

I — Inspections (§91.409)

  • Annual inspection — within the preceding 12 calendar months (all aircraft).
  • 100-hour (time-in-service) inspection required if:
    • Carrying a person for hire (other than crew member), or
    • Flight instructing for hire in an aircraft provided by the person giving the instruction.
    • "For hire" refers to the person, not the aircraft:
      • Flight school providing airplane + instructor for hire: 100-hours required
      • Student-owned aircraft: 100-hours not required.
      • Rental (no pilot or instructor): 100-hr not required.
    • The 100-hr inspection may be exceeded by up to 10 hours if the aircraft is enroute to a place where it can be done. That additional time must be included in computing the next 100-hours inspection.
    • An annual inspection can substitute for the 100-hour if done within 100 hours of time-in-service.
  • A progressive inspection schedule, if specifically approved by the FAA, may replace the annual and 100 hour inspections.

E — ELT (§91.207)

  • Inspected within the preceding 12 calendar months.
  • Battery must be replaced after more than 1 hour of cumulative transmitter use, or if 50% of its useful life has expired (or, for rechargeable batteries, 50% of the useful life of charge has expired).

What is a Departure Procedure, and what must you fly?AIM 5-2-9

A DP guarantees obstacle clearance only if you fly the assumptions: cross the departure end at 35 ft AGL, reach 400 ft AGL before turning, and climb at least 200 FPNM.

The obstacle-clearance assumptions

DPs ensure obstacle clearance, provided the aircraft:

  • crossed the departure end of the runway at least 35 ft AGL,
  • reaches 400 ft AGL before turning, and
  • climbs at least 200 Feet per NM (FPNM), or as published on the chart.

To convert FPNM to Feet-Per-Minute

FPM = FPNM X Groundspeed / 60

Pilots are encouraged to file and fly a DP at night, during marginal VMC or in IMC.

Two types of DP

  • Obstacle Departure Procedure (ODP)
    • Provides obstacle clearance only.
    • Printed either textually or graphically.
    • Graphic ODPs are titled "(OBSTACLE)."
  • Standard Instrument Departure (SID)
    • Provide obstacle clearance and reduces pilot/controller workload by simplifying ATC clearances and minimizing radio communications.
    • May include special radio failure procedures.
    • SIDs are always published graphically.

DPs are also categorized by required equipment

  • Non-RNAV DP — Uses ground-based navigation (e.g., VOR, DME, NDB).
  • RNAV DP — Require at least RNAV 1 performance. Identified with the word "RNAV" in the title.
  • RADAR DP — Uses ATC radar vectors to a route, NAVAID, or fix after departure. Annotated "RADAR REQUIRED."

What is a diverse departure, and what if there's no ODP?

When an IAP is built for an airport, every runway is assessed for departure obstacle clearance. If a runway passes, no ODP is published — but you still owe the standard climb.

  • When an Instrument Approach Procedure (IAP) is developed for an airport, each runway is assessed for departure obstacle clearance.
  • This assessment ensures an aircraft can climb and turn in any direction from a runway while maintaining proper obstacle clearance.

Pitot-Static Instruments

How the altimeter works and how to set it

The altimeter is an aneroid barometer that shows your height above a given pressure level, based on a standard pressure lapse rate of 1000' per inch of mercury.

Inside the instrument

  • A stack of sealed aneroid wafers expands and contracts with changes in atmospheric pressure received from the static port.
  • A mechanical linkage between the aneroid and the display translates the sensed pressure into an altitude indication.
  • An altimeter setting knob — on a "sensitive altimeter", which is what most aircraft altimeters are — lets you adjust the current pressure to the current altimeter setting published locally (available from ATIS, METAR or ATC).
  • The pressure setting is displayed in the "Kollsman Window" in mb and/or inches of mercury (Hg).

Setting rules in the US

  • Operating below 18,000' MSL: regularly set the altimeter to a station within 100 NM.
  • Above 18,000' MSL: set the standard sea level pressure of 29.92" Hg and operate in Flight Levels (FL).

Vertical Speed Indicator (VSI) — and why it lags

The VSI shows rate-of-climb in fpm, accurate only after a 6-9 second lag, plus rate trend, which appears immediately with a rate change.

  • As the diaphragm expands or contracts, a mechanical linkage moves the pointer needle to display your current rate of climb.

Airspeed Indicator (ASI) — how it works

The ASI measures the difference between impact (ram) air pressure from the pitot tube and ambient pressure from the static port. That difference is dynamic pressure, and it corresponds to airspeed.

A diaphragm in the instrument receives ram pressure from the pitot tube. The area outside the diaphragm is sealed and connected to the static port. A mechanical linkage converts the expansion and contraction of the diaphragm to the airspeed shown on the display dial.

How does GPS work, and how many satellites do you need?

GPS is a Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) operated by the United States. Your receiver measures distance to each satellite and intersects spheres to fix your position.

How the receiver gets a position

  • Your GPS receiver calculates the distance to a satellite from the time lapse between the broadcast timestamp (from an atomic clock onboard the satellite) and the time it received the signal.
  • With only one satellite you could virtually be on any point on a sphere surrounding it, with the calculated distance (the "pseudo-range") as the sphere's radius.
  • The receiver uses the intersection of spheres from multiple satellites to work out your geographical position. Course and speed are computed from changes in aircraft position.

RAIM is a function of GPS receivers that monitors the integrity of the satellite signals.

5Fault detectionRAIM needs a minimum of 5 satellites, or 4 satellites + an altimeter input (baro-aided RAIM)

  • A database loaded into the receiver holds navigational data: airports, navaids, routes, waypoints and instrument procedures.
  • Airborne GPS units use great-circle navigation.
  • GPS CDI deflection shows distance, unlike a VOR's CDI, which presents an angular distance off course in degrees.
  • GPS can substitute for ADF or DME, except for ADF substitution on NDB approaches without a GPS overlay ("or GPS" in the title).
  • Check GPS NOTAMs before the flight and use RAIM prediction if your receiver has it.

GPS augmentation systems, or Differential GPS (DGPS), improve GPS accuracy by measuring the errors seen by reference stations at known geographical locations and broadcasting those errors to supported receivers.

  • WAAS (Wide Area Augmentation System) in the US; EGNOS in Europe.
  • Ground stations — Wide-area Reference Stations and Wide-area Master Stations — measure GPS errors and produce correction signals. Those corrections are broadcast back up to the satellite segment, which bounces them back down to aircraft GPS WAAS receivers, improving accuracy, integrity and availability monitoring for GPS navigation.
  • Covers a wide area.
  • Facilitates APV approaches such as LPV and LNAV/VNAV, and non-precision LP approaches.

Ground Based Augmentation System (GBAS)

  • Formerly named Local Area Augmentation System (LAAS) in the US. Now replaced with the ICAO term "GBAS."
  • Errors are broadcast via VHF to GBAS-enabled GPS receivers.
  • GBAS is more accurate than WAAS but covers a much smaller geographical area.
  • Allows for category I and above approaches to GLS DA minima.

Calculate a VDP when it is not published

By distance: VDP (in NM from threshold) = MDH / 300

Example: MDH is 600 ft — how far is the VDP from the threshold?
VDP = 600 / 300 = 2 NM. Start the descent 2 NM from the threshold.

By time: MDH / 10 = seconds to subtract from the time between FAF and MAP

Example: MDH is 500 ft, FAF to MAP is 4:00 — when are you over the VDP and starting the descent from MDA/H?
500 / 10 = 50 seconds. 4:00 − 0:50 = 3:10. Start the descent at 3:10 (time from FAF).

Descent Planning Math

Rate of descent and top of descent for a 3° glide path

Two formulas do the whole job: VS = Ground Speed × 5, and TOD = Altitude to lose ÷ 300.

Rate of descent for a 3° glide path

VS (fpm) = Ground Speed × (10 / 2), or VS (fpm) = Ground Speed × 5

Example: 120 kts × (10 / 2) = 120 kts × 5 = 600 fpm

How far out to start a descent for a 3° glide path

TOD = Altitude to lose (ft) / 300

Example, on approach — 800 ft to lose from MDA to TCH:
800 / 300 = 2.67 NM. Start descent 2.67 NM from the runway threshold.

Example — cruising at FL350, ATC: "…cross LGA VOR at FL240":
Altitude to lose = 35,000 − 24,000 = 11,000 ft. 11000 / 300 = 36.67 NM. Start descent 36.67 NM from LGA VOR.

Other glide path angles — gradients and formulas

For anything other than 3°, work from the gradient: Descent gradient (%) = tan(descent angle) × 100.

What has to be present to form a thunderstorm?

Three conditions are required: moisture, an unstable lapse rate, and a lifting force.

1Sufficient water vaporMoisture.
2An unstable temperature lapse rateStability is the resistance of the atmosphere to upwards or downwards displacement. An unstable lapse rate allows any air mass displacement to further grow vertically.
3An initial uplifting forcee.g., front passages, orographic lifting by topography, heating from below, etc.
The three stages of a thunderstorm

Cumulus, Mature, Dissipating. All thunderstorm hazards are at their greatest intensity at the mature stage.

1Cumulus (3-5 mile height)The lifting action of the air begins; growth rate may exceed 3000 fpm.
2Mature (5-10 miles height)Begins when precipitation starts falling from the cloud base. Updraft at this stage may exceed 6000 fpm; downdrafts may exceed 2500 fpm. All thunderstorm hazards are at their greatest intensity at this stage.
3Dissipating (5-7 miles height)Characterized by strong downdrafts and the cell dying rapidly.
Memory aid (ours, not FAA) The stage that begins when rain reaches the ground is the same stage that is trying hardest to kill you — mature.

What are the four types of hypoxia?PHAK

Hypoxia is an insufficient supply of oxygen to the body cells. The guide splits it four ways by where the failure happens.

1Hypoxic hypoxiaInsufficient supply of O2 to the body as a whole. As altitude increases the O2 percentage of the atmosphere stays constant, but its pressure decreases. The reduced pressure becomes insufficient for the O2 molecules to pass through the respiratory system's membranes.
2Hypemic hypoxiaInability of the blood to carry the O2 molecules. May result from insufficient blood (bleeding or blood donation), anemia or CO poisoning.
3Histotoxic hypoxiaInability of the body cells to use the O2 supplied by the blood. Can be caused by use of alcohol or drugs.
4Stagnant hypoxiaCaused by the blood not flowing efficiently. Can be caused by heart problems, excessive acceleration (Gs), shock or a constricted blood vessel. Cold temperatures can restrict circulation and decrease blood supplied to the extremities.

Middle ear and sinus blockagePHAK

Air pressure in the middle ear and sinuses normally equalizes with outside air through the nasal passages. Block that path and the trapped air hurts — usually worst during descent.

  • Allergies, colds or sinus infections may block these small openings and prevent the pressure from equalizing.
  • If the air gets trapped it may cause extreme pain, reduction in hearing, or damage to the ear drums. This effect is usually most severe during descent.
  • To relieve it, try the "Valsalva Maneuver": pinch your nostrils and gently try to blow air out of your nose. This forces air through the Eustachian tube into the middle ear.
  • It may not work if you have a cold, sinus or ear infection, or a sore throat.
  • Consider seeing a physician if the condition doesn't clear after the flight.

Vestibular illusions — the six inner-ear trapsPHAK

These all come from fluid and otolith movement in the inner ear telling your brain something the airplane is not doing.

1The leansAfter leveling the wings following a prolonged turn, you may feel that the aircraft is banked in the opposite direction of the turn.
2Coriolis IllusionAfter a prolonged turn, the fluid in the ear canal moves at the same speed as the canal. A head movement on a different plane will cause the fluid to start moving and result in a false sensation of acceleration or turning on a different axis.
3Graveyard SpiralIn a prolonged, coordinated constant-rate turn you may get the illusion of not turning. After leveling the wings you may feel a turn in the other direction ("the leans"), so you turn back into the original direction. Since a higher angle of attack is required during a turn to remain level, you may notice a loss of altitude and pull back on the elevator — which may tighten the spiral and increase the loss of altitude.
4Somatogravic IllusionRapid acceleration stimulates the otolith organs the same way as tilting the head backwards, creating the illusion of a higher pitch angle. Deceleration causes the opposite — the sensation of tilting the head forward and the aircraft being nose-low.
5Inversion IllusionAn abrupt change from climb to straight and level may create the illusion of tumbling backwards, due to fluid movement in the otolith organs.
6Elevator IllusionAn abrupt upward vertical acceleration may create the illusion of climbing, due to fluid movement in the otolith organs.

Optical illusions on approach — the seven to knowPHAK

Most of these end the same way: you fly lower than you should. Runway width, slope, dark terrain, rain, haze and bright lights all bias your picture of the glidepath.

1Runway Width IllusionOn approach to land, a narrow runway may give the illusion that the airplane is too high; a wide runway may make it seem too low.
2Runway and Terrain Slope IllusionUp-sloping terrain or runway can give the illusion that the plane is higher than it actually is.
3Featureless Terrain IllusionAlso known as "black hole approach." Flying over dark or featureless terrain (for example, during an over-water approach) can give the illusion that the aircraft is at a higher altitude, causing you to fly lower than desired.
4Water RefractionLight refraction due to rain on the windshield may make the horizon seem lower and the aircraft appear higher. May lead you to fly lower than desired.
5HazeDuring an approach, haze may give the illusion that the runway is further away or that the airplane is higher than it is.
6FogFlying into fog may create an illusion of a nose-up motion.

How do you cope with spatial disorientation?PHAK

Eight steps from the handbook. The last one is the one that saves you: trust the instruments and disregard your sensory perceptions.

1Know the illusionsUnderstand the causes of the illusions that may affect you as a pilot and stay alert for them when flying.
2Get the weatherObtain and understand relevant preflight weather information.
3Stay proficientMaintain instrument proficiency and obtain training if needed before flying in marginal or instrument conditions.
4Stay outDo not fly into adverse weather conditions or into dark or featureless areas unless instrument proficient.
5Pick good referencesWhen using outside visual references, ensure they are reliable, fixed points on the earth's surface.
6Head stillAvoid sudden head movements, particularly during takeoff, turns, and approaches to landing.

The two high-intensity systems: ALSF-1 and ALSF-2

PAPI: what the four lights are telling you

A Precision Approach Path Indicator is a single row of four lights. Count the reds and whites. Charted with a circled P.

4 redToo low
3 red, 1 whiteSlightly low
2 red, 2 whiteOn correct approach path
1 red, 3 whiteSlightly high
4 whiteToo high

In the drawings the white lights appear on the side away from the runway and the reds nearest the runway, so the row turns white from the outside in as you go high.

Memory aid (ours, not FAA) The more white you see, the higher you are. Two and two is on path.
Two-bar VASI: three answers, not five

A 2-bar VASI gives you only too low, on correct approach path, or too high. Charted with a circled V.

The three indications (upper bar first, then lower bar)

Too lowUpper bar red, lower bar redBoth bars red.
On correct approach pathUpper bar red, lower bar white
Too highUpper bar white, lower bar whiteBoth bars white.

The three installations drawn

VASI 2One light in each barDimensions marked on the diagram: 700 ft and 800 ft.
VASI 4Two lights in each bar
VASI 12Three lights in each bar
Memory aid (ours, not FAA) All red is too low, all white is too high, red on top with white below is on path.
Three-bar VASI: two glide paths, four indications

A 3-bar VASI presents two visual glide paths. The lower path is typically set to 3°; the upper glide path, serving aircraft with taller cockpits, is set to about 0.25° higher. Charted with a circled V3.

The four indications (upper bar, middle bar, lower bar)

Below both glide pathsred / red / red
On lower glide pathred / red / white
On upper glide pathred / white / white
Above both glide pathswhite / white / white

The two installations drawn

VASI 6Two lights in each of the three bars
VASI 16Two lights in the upper bar, three in the middle bar, three in the lower bar

PASI: the one that pulses

A Pulsating Approach Slope Indicator is a single light source. You read the colour and whether it is pulsing or steady. Charted with a circled V2.

Above Glide PathPulsating White
On Glide PathSteady White or Alternating Red/White
Slightly Below Glide PathSteady Red
Below Glide PathPulsating Red
TRCV: amber, green, red

A Tri-Color Visual Approach Slope Indicator is a single light that changes colour. Charted with a circled V4.

Above Glide PathAmber
On Glide PathGreen

APAP: line up the panels

The Alignment of Elements System, or Approach Path Alignment Panels (APAP), is not a light-colour system at all — you fly it by getting the panels into a straight line. Charted with a circled V5.

Below glide pathElements out of line, centre panel low
On glide pathAll elements in a straight line
Above glide pathElements out of line, centre panel high
  • Consist of painted panels which may be lighted at night.
  • Pilot should position the aircraft so the elements are in alignment.
